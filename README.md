# Can Wikipedia page views predict next year's peak?

Daily English-Wikipedia page views of 12 topics (four holidays, two diseases, four events, two evergreen controls), 1 January 2016 to 30 September 2026: the open stand-in for Google search interest. Five questions:
- how seasonal is each topic, and does a holiday that moves (Easter, US Thanksgiving) break a model that uses the calendar date;
- which weekday is busiest, and how big is the weekday swing;
- how big are spikes, and how fast does attention fade after one (the half-life of the excess over the baseline);
- how well can the date and the height of next year's peak be forecast from earlier years only (walk-forward, 2019 to 2025);
- do related topics take attention from each other (displacement), or move together.

The questions follow GeeksforGeeks' "Google Search Analysis" (seasonality and spikes in search interest). Google Trends has no open API, so the data here is Wikipedia page views, which measure attention (readers who reached an article), not search interest. ## Data

The Wikimedia Pageviews REST API, `https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/metrics/pageviews/per-article/en.wikipedia/all-access/<agent>/<Article>/daily/20160101/20260930` (39 requests, one per article and agent, sequential with a one-second pause, with a User-Agent that names the project and its author; pulled 2026-10-04). The pageview data is released by the Wikimedia Foundation under CC0 (https://dumps.wikimedia.org/other/pageviews/readme.html); cite: Wikimedia Foundation, Pageviews API. The analysis uses agent `user` (traffic Wikimedia classifies as people's browsers): that excludes self-identified crawlers (`spider`) and, from 29 April 2020, traffic its classifier flags as `automated`; both are fetched too, only to measure what the filter removes. The raw answers are 22.7 MB (`data/`, not committed, regenerated by `download.py`, checksums in `results/pull.json`). Because the data is CC0 and small, `counts/daily_views.csv` (0.65 MB, 3,926 days by 39 article-and-agent columns) is committed, so `check.py` runs without downloading anything.

## Steps

1. **Download** 12 topics with agent `user`, the same with `automated` and `spider`, and three redirect titles (`Olympics`, `Flu`, `2024_solar_eclipse`) to see what a redirect captures.
2. **Audit.** Days, missing days, zeros, the first day of automated traffic, and the share of automated and crawler views by year.
3. **Weekday effects.** The log of daily views minus its 7-day centred mean, averaged by weekday.
4. **Seasonality** (complete years 2016 to 2025). Each day is predicted from the same calendar date in the other nine years (leave one year out), after removing the weekday effect and each year's level: the seasonal R-squared. For Easter and Thanksgiving the same score over the 61 days around the holiday, by calendar date and by days from the holiday.
5. **Spikes.** A day with at least 3,000 views and at least 5 times the median of the previous 28 days starts an event; baseline = that median; the excess = views minus baseline from the peak; `t10` = days until the excess is 10% of its peak value; half-life = ln 2 over the slope of a least-squares line through the log excess, kept only for the 21 events with at least four days above the floor and R-squared of at least 0.8.
6. **Walk-forward** (2019 to 2025, each year forecast only from the years before it): the peak date by last year's date, the peak of the average year, and (for Easter and Thanksgiving) the holiday rule; the peak height by last year's peak, the geometric mean of earlier peaks, last year's median times the usual peak-to-median ratio, and that with last year's trend.
7. **Displacement.** Weekly sums (560 weeks), the year-on-year change of the log of each week, Pearson (and Spearman) correlations of all 66 pairs with a Bartlett effective sample size and a Fisher interval, and a partial version that removes each topic's regression on the average change of the two evergreen topics.
8. **Check.** `check.py` recomputes every table in plain Python (json, csv, math and loops, no pandas or numpy), the two holiday dates by a different algorithm each: 4,775 numbers, to a relative tolerance of 1e-9.
9. **Charts.**

## Results

- **Holidays repeat, events do not.** The seasonal R-squared is 0.97 for Halloween, 0.96 for Christmas, 0.94 for Thanksgiving and 0.78 for Easter, 0.62 for Eurovision, 0.45 for influenza and 0.25 for measles; it is 0.12 for the World Cup, 0.07 for the Olympics, and -0.04 and -0.05 for the evergreen Japan and Chicago. The busiest week of Halloween has 82 times the views of its quietest, Thanksgiving 61, Christmas 35, Easter 23.
- **A moving date breaks a calendar model.** Easter's date moved across 25 days in 2016 to 2025; around the holiday, predicting a day from the same calendar date gives R-squared 0.33, from the same offset from Easter 0.94. For Thanksgiving (a 6-day span) it is 0.86 against 0.97. The peak is on the holiday itself in the median year (offset 0 days for both).
- **Saturday is the quietest day.** For 10 of the 12 topics (the World Cup is quietest on Friday and Eurovision, whose final is on a Saturday, on Wednesday); the swing between the busiest and the quietest weekday is 6% (World Cup) to 28% (Thanksgiving), 17% at the median topic. Days are UTC.
- **Spikes are short.** 107 spikes in all: in 76 of them half the excess is gone the day after the peak, 42 are down to 10% of it within two days and 74 within three. Only 21 have a clean exponential decay with at least four days to fit; their half-life has a median of 1.1 days (range 0.9 to 5.7), but this sample leaves out every faster spike. Scheduled events (the four holidays, 54 spikes) fade because the calendar moves on: 53 of them lose half the next day.
- **Long ones are events, not decay.** The Olympics' spikes take 17 to 20 days to fall to 10% in 6 of 7 cases, about the length of the Games, and their exponential fits are poor (R-squared 0.26 to 0.48). 10 of Thanksgiving's 20 spikes fall on the second Monday of October, Canadian Thanksgiving, in the same article.
- **Next year's peak date is known for holidays, not for events.** For the five annual topics the mean miss of the peak date is 5.8 days using last year's date (0 for Halloween and Christmas) against 81 days for the other seven (a random date would miss by 91). Easter misses by 12.4 days using last year's date and by 0 with the holiday rule, which is calendar knowledge, not statistics.
- **Its height is off by about a quarter at best.** The mean absolute log error of the peak height is 0.23 for the annual topics with last year's peak (26% off) and 0.24 with last year's median times the usual ratio; for the other seven topics it is 0.92 at best (a factor of 2.5).
- **Related topics move together, they do not displace each other.** The mean correlation of the weekly year-on-year changes is 0.24 for the 8 related pairs (0.36 by Spearman, 0.21 after the common factor) and 0.01 for the 37 other pairs; 5 of the 8 related intervals are above zero. 1 of the 66 pairs (Easter and influenza, r = -0.12) has an interval entirely below zero, about what chance gives.
- **What the filter leaves.** The flagged automated class is 2% of the `user` views in 2020 and 23% in 2023 (12 topics pooled), crawlers 31% in 2025; there is no automated class before 29 April 2020, so earlier `user` counts include traffic that would now be flagged. From January to September 2023 to 2026 the median topic (all but the World Cup) fell to 0.63 of its views and to 0.49 of its 2016 level, with Christmas at 0.48 and Thanksgiving at 0.45 of 2023.

## Not done, and caveats

- Page views measure attention to Wikipedia, not interest in the Google sense: a searcher can be satisfied by a snippet and never click, and Wikipedia's traffic depends on search engines sending readers. Google Trends was not used (no open API), so nothing here compares the two.
- `agent=user` removes self-identified crawlers and (from 29 April 2020) flagged automated traffic, not bots that look like people, and it changes definition on that date. The leak is visible: flagged automated views were 82% of the `user` views of Chicago in 2025 and 145% of Thanksgiving's from January to September 2026. Part of the fall in levels since 2016 may be reclassification, not fewer readers; search engines and AI answers keeping readers off the site is a hypothesis this data cannot test.
- Redirects: the titles `Olympics`, `Flu` and `2024_solar_eclipse` hold 2.0%, 2.4% and 0.7% of the views of their canonical articles; one redirect per topic was measured, so the true leak is larger. The eclipse title drew views long before 2024 (a median of 329 a day over the whole period) and spikes on the dates of neighbouring eclipses (365,407 views on 21 August 2017, 29,213 on 14 October 2023, 15,595 on 12 August 2026): spillover from other eclipses, not the 2024 event.
- Calendar disruptions: Eurovision 2020 was cancelled (its 27 June 2020 spike of 65,782 views is unexplained here), the Olympics of 2020 were held in 2021, the 2022 World Cup was in November and December. 2026 ends on 30 September, so it is used in the levels, the audit and the spikes but not in the seasonality or the walk-forward, and its Halloween, Thanksgiving and Christmas are not yet in the data.
- The half-life is not a general result: scheduled spikes fade because the calendar moves on, the clean fits leave out all spikes with fewer than four days above the floor, and unscheduled news spikes are too few here to give a half-life of their own.
- Walk-forward has at most 7 forecasts per topic (2019 to 2025), and 12 topics were chosen by hand, partly because they are seasonal; the results do not describe Wikipedia in general. The forecasts use only earlier years. The spike settings (5 times the baseline, 3,000 views, four days to fit, R-squared 0.8) were set after a first look at the spike list, to drop noise events and meaningless fits; no other setting was tuned.
- Correlations of related topics show association, not displacement or cause; 66 pairs is a small family, and the pair groups were fixed by me before the correlations were computed.
- English Wikipedia only, one article per topic, all-access (desktop, mobile web and app together), one flagged-agent filter.
